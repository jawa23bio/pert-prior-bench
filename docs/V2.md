# v2: review of the benchmark and what changed

This note has three parts: what the first version got right, what limits its
conclusion (each with the evidence), and how to run the v2 protocol that fixes
those limits. Nothing in v1 was removed: `scripts/run_benchmark.py` still gives
the committed synthetic results (`pytest` checks this), and its defaults are unchanged.

**Status of the numbers.** Everything quoted from "v1" below was recomputed from
the per-perturbation CSVs committed in this repo. The v2 protocol was then run
on the real pseudobulk file (`data/norman_pb.h5ad`, which reproduces the
committed v1 results to 1e-7); outputs are in `results_v2/`, `results_v2_direct/`,
`results_spike_in/` and `results_spike_in_dropout/`. The noise ceiling (3.2) was
run on the cell-level file; its output is in `results_noise_ceiling/`.

## 0. What v2 found on Norman et al.

Scores are the centered metric (`pearson_ctr_top20`; 0 = no better than the mean
of the training perturbations), 5 x 5-fold CV, on the 34 perturbed TFs that have
at least one measured target in the network.

| model | score [95% CI over perturbations] |
|---|---|
| mean baseline | 0 (by definition) |
| `linear[expression]` (no network) | 0.209 [0.055, 0.357] |
| `linear[both]`, real network | -0.030 [-0.177, 0.121] |
| `linear[both]`, regulator-permuted network (20 draws) | 0.082 (draws range -0.046 to 0.169) |
| `linear[both]`, target-permuted network (20 draws) | -0.028 |
| `direct[prior]`, real network | -0.002 |
| `direct[prior]`, regulator-permuted network (99 draws) | -0.005 (draws range -0.053 to 0.031) |

1. **The expression-only model does predict something perturbation-specific for
   TFs**, which the v1 metric hid (there it was +0.007 above the mean). 20 of 34
   TFs score above 0 (Wilcoxon p = 0.019), the mean is 0.13 to 0.27 in each of
   the five CV repeats, and the best-predicted TFs are the myeloid ones (CEBPA
   0.90, FOSB 0.87, CEBPB 0.86, CEBPE 0.78, SPI1 0.75). It is modest: the 71
   perturbations without network coverage score 0.048 [-0.070, 0.168], and MSE is
   no better than the mean for any model.
2. **Adding the network removes that signal instead of adding to it.**
   `linear[both]` with the real network scores below 18 of the 20
   regulator-permuted draws (one-sided p for "real is better" = 0.90). The real
   network is not distinguishable from a wrong one, and if anything sits at the
   low end. The target-permuted null gives the same score as the real network,
   so the damage comes from the network features themselves, not from which
   genes they point at.
3. **Read literally, the network's edges predict nothing here.** `direct[prior]`
   scores -0.002 against a null of -0.005 (p = 0.55 with 99 draws). Its one fitted
   scale is clipped to zero in 92% of fits; left free it is slightly negative
   (mean -0.022), meaning annotated targets move, if anything, against the
   annotated sign.
4. **That negative has a detection limit.** A planted network-shaped effect one
   tenth the size of each perturbation's own specific effect is detected by
   `direct[prior]` (beta >= 0.1, with or without 30% of edges removed). The v1
   analysis needed beta >= 1.0, and with 30% of edges removed it detected nothing
   up to beta = 1. See 3.3.
5. **There is a lot left to predict.** Splitting each perturbation's cells in
   half and scoring one half against the other gives 0.97 on the same centered
   metric (0.98 for the 34 covered TFs), an estimated ceiling of about 0.99
   against the full data. The specific effects are highly reproducible; the
   best model here reaches 0.21. Noise is not why the network fails.
6. **Uncertainty.** The v1 interval covers 10.2% of outcomes for
   `linear[expression]`; intervals from out-of-fold residuals cover 80% at a
   half-width of 0.079, the same for every model. No model earns narrower honest
   intervals, and the model's own uncertainty does not rank its errors
   (Spearman 0.03).

Caveats: 34 TFs in one CRISPRa screen; several comparisons were made and the
p-values are not corrected for that; the spike-in plants its effect along the
same propagated rows the models are given, which is the favourable case; and
the noise ceiling measures reproducibility between cells of this one
experiment (both halves share its batches), not between experiments.

## 1. What v1 got right

- A matched ablation: one model, only the source of the perturbation embedding changes.
- A negative control and a positive control, both run through the same code.
- Nested selection of the ridge penalty, so the test fold is never used for tuning.
- Per-perturbation outputs committed, which is what made this review possible.
- It reproduces: `bash run_synthetic.sh` regenerates `results_synthetic/` (identical up to
  floating-point noise around 1e-8 across numpy versions).
- It reports its own failed check (the "shared response removed" run) instead of hiding it.

## 2. What limits the conclusion

### 2.1 No model beats the mean, so the comparison has no headroom

`linear[expression]` minus the mean baseline is +0.007 (95% CI -0.006 to +0.020)
over the 105 singles, and the two are correlated 0.99 across perturbations. The
score of about 0.52 is the response shared by all perturbations, which the mean
baseline already contains. Differences between models on `pearson_top20` are
therefore differences in how much noise each model adds to the mean, not in how
much perturbation-specific biology it predicts.

This is a known property of control-referenced metrics (Viñas Torné et al. 2025;
Ahlmann-Eltze et al. 2025; Miller et al. 2025). On the v1 metric nothing,
including the network, beat the mean, which is a weaker and different statement
from "the network carries no usable information". The centered metric then
showed the v1 metric was also hiding real signal in the expression-only model
(section 0).

**v2:** the primary metric is centered on the training mean
(`pearson_ctr_top20`): correlation of (truth - training mean) with
(prediction - training mean). The mean baseline scores 0 by definition, so any
positive number is perturbation-specific signal.

### 2.2 The "shared response removed" check was biased, not uninformative

`make_variants.py` subtracts the mean of all singles, including the held-out
ones. Inside each CV fold the training mean is then anti-correlated with the
test perturbations by construction, which is why every model scored below zero
(mean baseline -0.19). `tests/test_pertbench.py` reproduces the artefact on pure
noise. Centering on the training mean inside each fold removes it.

### 2.3 The shuffled control is closer to "no network" than "wrong network"

`shuffled()` relabels every gene. Only a few percent of measured genes are
regulators, so after the relabel a perturbed TF almost always lands on a
non-regulator and gets an all-zero prior row. In the synthetic control only 16
of the 60 covered TFs keep any targets after the shuffle; on the real Norman
gene set it is 2 of 34. Consistent with that, in the committed
Norman results the shuffled model tracks `linear[expression]` almost exactly
(per-perturbation correlation 0.991 on the 43 covered TFs; mean difference
-0.009, CI -0.026 to +0.006).

So "a shuffled network did as well" mostly says "no network did as well". There
was also a single shuffle (seed 1), so no null distribution.

**v2:** two nulls that keep every TF a regulator, 20 draws each by default, with
an empirical p-value: `regulator-permuted` (TFs swap target sets, so each TF
has the wrong targets) and `target-permuted` (each TF keeps its number of
targets and its overlap with other TFs, but the targets are random genes).
`run_v2.py` also prints how many covered TFs the v1 control left with targets
on your gene set.

### 2.4 The network made things worse, which points at the model

On the 34 TFs with a measured target, `linear[both]` minus `linear[expression]`
is -0.048 (CI -0.088 to -0.015, Wilcoxon p = 0.0008; on all 43 CollecTRI
regulators it is -0.043, p = 0.0001). An uninformative feature under a
well-tuned ridge should be neutral. 73% of the drop comes from five TFs
(KMT2A -0.51, KLF1 -0.22, CEBPE -0.22, ETS2 -0.13, RREB1 -0.11). Three things in
the model contribute:

- **Feature scaling.** Each feature is divided by its largest training value. A
  prior feature that is tiny in training but not for a held-out TF gets
  multiplied by a huge factor. In two robustness runs this produced
  per-perturbation MSEs up to 36 (`targets`, shuffled) and 214 (`noshared`,
  `linear[both]`; 12 perturbations more than 10x the baseline), against a normal
  value near 0.006. Those two summary rows are numerical blow-ups.
- **Shrinkage in the wrong place.** The same ridge penalty is applied to the
  projection onto the output basis. With an orthonormal basis that just scales
  every prediction by 1/(1+lambda). Pearson does not notice, but MSE and interval
  coverage do, and lambda is chosen by Pearson.
- **A three-value grid** (0.1, 1, 10).

**v2:** block scaling (one scale per feature block), no shrinkage on the output
projection, ridge chosen by held-out MSE over 0.01 to 1000. The old behaviour
remains the default of `LinearModel`, so v1 still reproduces.

### 2.5 The model reads the network only indirectly

`linear[prior]` never uses "TF g regulates gene j" as a prediction. It embeds
each TF by a rank-50 SVD of the propagation matrix over all regulators and
learns a 50 x 50 map from about 34 covered training TFs. Two consequences,
both now measured by `run_v2.py` before any model is fitted:

- the SVD is dominated by hub TFs, so part of a perturbed TF's own target set is
  truncated away (`retained`);
- a held-out TF can only be predicted through directions that training TFs
  share (`transferable`).

How much this matters is an empirical question, answered by the spike-in (3.3):
on the real data v1 only notices a planted network effect once it is as large
as the perturbation-specific effect itself, and not at all if 30% of the edges
are missing. It is far less sensitive than a direct test, though not blind.

**v2:** adds `direct[prior]`: effect(g) = training mean + alpha x (g's network
row), with one fitted number. If that cannot beat the mean and cannot be told
apart from a regulator-permuted network, the edges themselves are not
predictive here, and "a linear model cannot use it" is no longer an available
explanation.

### 2.6 The 10% coverage is true but is not a finding about the data

The interval was prediction +/- 1.28 x (bootstrap spread of the fitted mean). A
bootstrap over training perturbations measures how much the fit moves, not how
far a new perturbation falls from the fit. It leaves out the residual error,
which is nearly all of the error (12x larger than the bootstrap spread in the
synthetic control). It is a confidence interval for the mean used as a
prediction interval, so it under-covers by construction. The mean baseline
looked calibrated (0.83) only because its "std" is the spread of training
effects, which is a predictive spread.

**v2:** `--calibrate` builds intervals from out-of-fold residuals (split
conformal, CV+ style). Coverage comes out at 0.80 on both test datasets. With
coverage fixed, the informative quantities are the interval width and whether
the model's own uncertainty ranks its errors (`err_vs_unc`).

### 2.7 One split, no noise ceiling, no sensitivity estimate

- One 5-fold split. The covered TFs' mean score ranges from about 0.1 in one
  fold to 0.76 in another. v2 uses repeated, stratified folds and reports
  confidence intervals over perturbations.
- No estimate of the best achievable score. `scripts/noise_ceiling.py` gives
  the split-half reliability of each perturbation's effect.
- No estimate of how large an effect the benchmark could detect.
  `scripts/spike_in.py` plants one and reports the detection limit.

### 2.8 "43 covered" counts 9 TFs the network says nothing about

v1 reports coverage as the share of perturbed genes that are CollecTRI sources
(43 of 105). Nine of those 43 have no target among the 2,085 measured genes
(CBFA2T3, FOXF1, HES7, HOXB9, ISL2, MAML2, OSR2, RUNX1T1, ZBTB10), so their prior
row is empty and no model can use the network for them. 34 TFs have at least
one measured target (median 5; 18 have five or more).

v1's split tables (`in_prior=True`) already used the 34, because
`run_benchmark.py` splits on whether the prior row is non-zero, but the README
labelled those columns "43 in prior" and "62 not in prior". The numbers were
for 34 and 71. The README and `robustness/README.md` now say so. v2 defines
coverage by measured targets throughout.

### 2.9 The positive control is easier than the real problem

The synthetic network is dense (60 TFs x 15 targets among 300 genes, heavy
overlap after propagation) and the responses are generated by the same
propagation rule the model uses. It shows the code can find an effect. It does
not show the analysis would find a realistic one in a network where a covered
TF has a median of 4 measured targets. The spike-in on the real data is the
matched positive control.

## 3. Running v2 on the real screen

Inputs are the files you already have (`data/norman_pb.h5ad`, `data/collectri.csv`).

### 3.1 Main run (about 6 minutes on 2 cores, under 1 GB of memory)

```bash
python scripts/run_v2.py --h5ad data/norman_pb.h5ad --already-log --min-cells 1 \
    --sep _ --n-hvg 2085 --prior data/collectri.csv --calibrate --out results_v2
```

For final numbers raise the null draws; the smallest possible p is 1/(1+draws).
`direct[prior]` alone is fast (about 3 minutes, 400 MB peak with 99 draws):

```bash
python scripts/run_v2.py ... --n-null 99 --no-linear-prior --out results_v2_direct
```

`summary.md` then answers, in order:

| section | question |
|---|---|
| Before fitting anything | How many targets does the network give each perturbed TF, how much survives the SVD, how much can transfer, and how many TFs did the v1 control leave with targets? |
| `pearson_ctr_top20` | Does any model predict perturbation-specific effects (above 0)? |
| Real vs null | Is the real network better than a wrong one of the same shape? |
| Paired differences | Does reading the network beat not reading it, per TF? |
| Uncertainty | Are honest intervals narrower for any model, and does any model know where it is wrong? |

### 3.2 Noise ceiling (needs the cell-level file)

```bash
python scripts/noise_ceiling.py --h5ad data/norman.h5ad --sep _ \
    --genes-from data/norman_pb.h5ad --stage1 data/norman_pb.h5ad.stage1.npz \
    --prior data/collectri.csv --out results_noise_ceiling
```

The file is read once for all splits (under a minute for the 698 MB Norman
file). Add `--checkpoint some.npz` to resume an interrupted read.

| metric | half vs half | estimated ceiling |
|---|---|---|
| `pearson_ctr_top20`, all 105 singles | 0.969 | 0.992 |
| `pearson_ctr_top20`, 34 covered TFs | 0.981 | 0.995 |
| `pearson_ctr_all`, all 105 singles | 0.770 | 0.933 |
| `pearson_top20` (v1 metric), all 105 singles | 0.959 | 0.990 |

The least reproducible perturbation is PLK4 (0.54, 113 cells); reliability
rises with cell count (correlation with log cells 0.44).

### 3.3 Detection limit (about 6 minutes with the defaults below)

```bash
python scripts/spike_in.py --h5ad data/norman_pb.h5ad --already-log --min-cells 1 \
    --sep _ --n-hvg 2085 --prior data/collectri.csv --out results_spike_in
python scripts/spike_in.py ... --edge-dropout 0.3 --out results_spike_in_dropout
```

Smallest beta detected (tried 0.1, 0.25, 0.5, 1), where beta is the planted
effect size relative to each perturbation's own specific effect:

| analysis | Norman, full network | Norman, 30% of edges removed |
|---|---|---|
| v1 (`linear[both]` vs `linear[expression]`, one split) | 1.0 | not detected |
| v2 `linear[both]` vs regulator-permuted null | 0.5 | 0.25 |
| v2 `direct[prior]` vs regulator-permuted null | 0.1 | 0.1 |

No analysis fired at beta = 0. With 19 null draws the smallest possible p is
0.05, so "detected" means the real network beat every draw.

### 3.4 How to read the outcome

| what you see | what you can say |
|---|---|
| `direct[prior]` centered score > 0 and above both nulls | The edges are predictive; v1 missed it because of how the model read them. |
| `direct[prior]` = nulls, spike-in limit small, noise ceiling clearly above 0 | A solid negative: the network's edges do not predict these responses, and an effect of size beta >= X would have been seen. |
| `direct[prior]` = nulls, but noise ceiling near 0 or spike-in limit large | Undetermined: this screen cannot answer the question at this cell count / coverage. |
| real > regulator-permuted but real = target-permuted (for `linear[both]`) | The network knows which TFs act alike, not which genes they act on. |

Norman lands in the second row: `direct[prior]` equals its nulls, the detection
limit is beta = 0.1, and the ceiling is about 0.99.

## 5. Things to take into account next

Roughly in order of how much they would change the conclusion.

1. **A screen where the prior has a chance.** Norman perturbs 105 genes picked
   for growth phenotypes; 43 are TFs with a median of 4 measured targets. A
   TF-focused screen changes that: the Replogle et al. 2022 genome-scale K562
   CRISPRi screen (many TFs, knockdown, where a direct target should fall), or
   the Joung et al. 2023 TF atlas (overexpression of TF ORFs in hESCs).
2. **A network from the same cell type.** K562 is among the most heavily
   profiled lines in ENCODE. TF ChIP-seq peaks near promoters in K562 give a
   context-matched prior to compare against CollecTRI with the same code.
3. **A measured-response prior.** Ahlmann-Eltze et al. report that the one thing
   that reliably helped a linear model was a perturbation embedding learned from
   another perturbation dataset. Using K562 CRISPRi responses of the same genes
   as the "prior" for CRISPRa is the strongest available comparator: if even that
   barely helps, a curated network was never going to.
4. **Non-TF perturbations.** 62 of 105 perturbed genes are not regulators in the
   network, so a TF network cannot help them by design. A pathway or
   protein-interaction prior (GO, STRING, as in GEARS) covers them.
5. **Gene selection.** Variable genes are chosen using all cells, including the
   held-out perturbations (a mild leak), and most CollecTRI targets are not among
   them. Decide the gene set from control cells or training perturbations only,
   and report results on an expressed-gene set as well as HVGs.
6. **Direction and dose.** CRISPRa strength differs per guide; the realised
   on-target fold change is in the data. Targets agree with the edge sign 43% of
   the time against a 38% baseline, so the sign information is weak here.
7. **Doubles.** The additive baseline has seen both singles, so the only thing
   left to predict is the deviation from additivity. Score that, with "shrunken
   additive" as the baseline (sub-additivity is common and not specific to a pair).
8. **Control noise.** Every effect is measured against the same control mean, so
   control sampling noise is shared by all perturbations and is "predicted" by
   the mean baseline. With many control cells this is small; check it by
   splitting the non-targeting guides.
9. **Housekeeping.** Add a LICENSE (without one nobody may reuse the code), a
   CITATION file, and check the redistribution terms of the 4 MB OmniPath export
   in `data/` (CollecTRI aggregates 12 resources with their own licences; the
   derived CSV plus the download command may be the safer thing to ship).
